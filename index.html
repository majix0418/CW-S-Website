China, Taiwan, and the Olympics

Cold War & Sports

How did the presence of two Chinas in the Olympics affect the sporting community and relations between the two countries?

Background

The 1949 split

Source notes contain quotations and paraphrases. Analysis marks my interpretation.

Taiwan / ROC

Republic of China

Background and source notes

  • In 1949, after the civil war between the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the Chinese Nationalists (KMT), the KMT retreated to Taiwan.
  • Most members of the former Chinese Olympic Committee (COC) fled with the Republic of China (ROC) government. (Chan 473)
  • The ROC retained China’s seat in the United Nations after its government moved to Taiwan.

Analysis

  • Having former COC members gave the ROC an early advantage with the International Olympic Committee (IOC) over the newly established PRC.
  • Did this help explain the IOC’s early preference for ROC participation?

China / PRC

People’s Republic of China

Background and source notes

  • The People’s Republic of China (PRC) was established on the mainland in 1949.
  • The China National Amateur Athletic Foundation remained in Nanjing after many sports delegates fled to Taiwan. (Chan 474)
  • In October 1949, it was reorganized as the All-China Athletic Federation and claimed jurisdiction over all Chinese Olympic activities. (Chan 474)
  • The PRC’s claim included Taiwan. Its account accused Chiang Kai-shek’s clique of trying to create two Chinas or one China, one Taiwan in international organizations. (“‘Two Chinas’”)

Analysis

  • Calling the Nationalists a clique shows the PRC’s dislike of them and its unwillingness to come together with them.
  • The PRC’s claim to control all Chinese Olympic affairs likely drew opposition from the ROC committee and the IOC.

Timeline

Who represents China?

The dispute played out in invitations, withdrawals, votes, and decisions about the names and symbols athletes could compete under.

  1. 1952

    Olympic Games

    Participation becomes a political question

    Source notes

    • The PRC said that the main obstacle to China’s participation in the 1952 Olympic Games was in essence a political problem. (“‘Two Chinas’”)
    • It called the problem an obstacle put up by persons who were hostile to the newly-born People’s Republic of China. (“‘Two Chinas’”)

    Analysis

    • The PRC blamed its inability to participate on people it believed were hostile to its government.
    • What reasons would the PRC give for the IOC treating it differently from the USSR?
  2. 1954

    May · Athens

    The IOC recognizes both committees

    Source notes

    • At its 50th session in Athens, the IOC voted 23–21 to recognize the National Olympic Committees (NOCs) in Beijing and Taipei. (People’s Republic)
    • The IOC recognized Beijing’s body as the Olympic Committee of the Chinese Republic and Taipei’s as the Chinese Olympic Committee. In 1957, the IOC listed Beijing’s body as the Olympic Committee of the People’s Democratic Republic of China. (People’s Republic)

    Analysis

    • The close 23–21 vote suggests that both committees had nearly equal support in the IOC at the time.

    PRC response

    Beijing objects to dual recognition

    Source notes

    • The PRC presented the recognition of both committees without prior discussion as a plot to divide China. Its account said, This was a sheer plot to create ‘two Chinas.’ (“‘Two Chinas’”)

    Analysis

    • The PRC used the vote as evidence for its claim that the IOC was hostile to it.
  3. 1955

    What counts as politics?

    Source notes

    • According to the PRC’s account, an IOC representative argued that the issue the PRC was protesting had nothing to do with sports. (“‘Two Chinas’”)
    • The PRC portrayed his argument as unreasonable and hypocritical. It responded, Actually, he was playing a most dirty political trick himself under the cloak of ‘apolitics,’ which made it all the more deceitful. (“‘Two Chinas’”)

    Analysis

    • The PRC’s wording shifted blame to the IOC. Its response made a political grievance central to a dispute about Olympic participation.
  4. 1956

    November

    An invitation ends in a withdrawal

    Source notes

    • Beijing’s NOC accepted its invitation on November 20. Taipei’s NOC then declined, reversed its decision, and chose to compete. Beijing withdrew in protest. (People’s Republic)
    • After withdrawing, the PRC continued to demand Taiwan’s expulsion. (Chan 474)

    Analysis

    • The exchange shows how each committee’s decision depended on what the other did. Taipei ultimately agreed to compete alongside Beijing, and Beijing chose to withdraw.
    • The PRC’s limited influence at the time may explain why the protest had little effect.
  5. 1958

    The PRC committee breaks with the IOC

    Source notes

    • The PRC’s Chinese Olympic Committee severed relations with the IOC in 1958, as described in the 2020 account. (International Olympic Committee)
    • Cheung links Taiwan’s earlier withdrawal in protest to the row that led the PRC to leave the IOC and miss the Games for the next 32 years. (Cheung)

    Analysis

    • Cheung’s description of the PRC as quitting frames its withdrawal as a loss.
  6. 1962

    Summer · Fourth Asian Games

    Indonesia rejects the ROC name

    Source notes

    • Indonesia, the host of the Fourth Asian Games, refused to invite Taiwan under the name Republic of China. (“‘Two Chinas’”)
    • The PRC said it adopted a tit-for-tat policy to break what it called a blockade. It described the strategy as setting up a separate kitchen or putting on a rival show. (“‘Two Chinas’”)

    Analysis

    • The word blockade evokes an act of war and makes the PRC’s response sound forced by the IOC.
    • Why did Indonesia take this position? What role, if any, did PRC pressure or persuasion play?
  7. 1971

    October · United Nations

    The PRC takes China’s UN seat

    Source notes

    • The PRC took China’s seat at the United Nations, replacing the ROC’s representatives. (Chan 475)
    • After the change, Beijing regarded its existing relationship with the IOC as abnormal and unjust because the PRC now held the UN seat. (Chan 477)

    Analysis

    • The PRC gained a permanent seat on the Security Council, giving it more international recognition and more leverage with the IOC.
    • Beijing’s appeal to its UN status used political power in the dispute it had accused the IOC of politicizing.
  8. 1972

    August · 20th Olympics

    Beijing declines an observer invitation

    Source notes

    • The organizers of the 20th Olympics sought to invite an observer delegation from the PRC. The PRC declined because the ROC was participating. (Chan 476)
    • The PRC saw the invitation as reflecting an improvement in its relations with the Federal Republic of Germany. (Chan 476)

    Analysis

    • Beijing gave up an opportunity to build ties in order to oppose a two-Chinas arrangement.
    • The PRC still saw the invitation as a sign of better relations with Germany.
  9. 1973

    The Asian Games Federation admits the PRC

    Source notes

    • After the PRC expanded its international ties, the Asian Games Federation’s executive committee decided to admit the PRC and deny the ROC. (Chan 476)
    • The IOC did not carry out its threat, concerned about alienating members of the Olympic movement who favored PRC participation. (Chan 477)

    Analysis

    • The IOC’s earlier recognition of both committees came under pressure. For many supporters of PRC participation, recognizing the PRC meant excluding the ROC.
  10. 1975

    Beijing builds support across sports federations

    Source notes

    • The PRC encountered difficulties joining international sports federations where Taiwan was already a member. (Chan 476)
    • By 1975, the PRC belonged to nine federations and was ready to rejoin the IOC. (Chan 477)

    Analysis

    • The federations faced their own choices about recognition. The PRC’s rapid gains suggest more sports organizations had begun to favor its participation.
  11. 1976

    Montreal Olympics

    Canada restricts Taiwan’s competition name

    Source notes

    • After considering the PRC’s request to deny the ROC entry, Canada required Taiwanese athletes to compete without using the name China. (Chan 478)
    • The IOC proposed that Taiwan march as Taiwan-ROC behind a flag bearing the Olympic rings. (Chan 478)
    • The United States considered boycotting the Olympics over Taiwan’s treatment and ultimately chose to participate. (Chan 478)

    Analysis

    • Canada’s decision showed the PRC’s growing influence with host countries.
    • The IOC’s compromise gave Taiwan a way to participate under a distinct name. The name conflicted with both governments’ views of who represented China.
    • The U.S. decision showed the limits of the support Taiwan could expect from a longtime ally.
  12. 1979

    The U.S. changes diplomatic ties

    Source notes

    • The United States broke diplomatic ties with Taiwan and established diplomatic relations with the PRC. (Cheung)

    Analysis

    • Taiwan lost the backing of one of its biggest supporters, shifting the dispute in the PRC’s favor.

    April

    The IOC maintains recognition of both committees

    Source notes

    • Citing the Olympic spirit and the Olympic Charter, the IOC resolved to recognize the Chinese Olympic Committee in Beijing (then Peking) and maintain recognition of the Chinese Olympic Committee in Taipei. (People’s Republic)
    • The IOC Executive Board revised the wording for Taipei to to maintain recognition of the Olympic Committee located in Taipei. (People’s Republic)

    Analysis

    • The IOC appeared to maintain a neutral position by recognizing both. The shared name left open which committee would represent China and what status the other would hold.
    • Removing Chinese from the Taipei committee’s name signaled that the IOC no longer treated it as the main committee for China.
  13. 1979

    Taiwan rejects direct negotiations

    Source notes

    • The KMT insisted on no contact, no negotiation, no compromise. (Cheung)
    • Chan argues that Taiwan’s refusal to negotiate directly with the PRC preserved its position in the short term and pushed the IOC and PRC to work on a solution without consulting it. (Chan 479)

    Analysis

    • Why did Taiwan refuse direct negotiations when it still had the advantage of being a longstanding IOC member?

    Later in 1979

    The Chinese Taipei formula

    Source notes

    • The IOC voted to recognize the Chinese Olympic Committee and the Chinese Taipei Olympic Committee, with both entitled to participate in Olympic activities. (Chan 481)
    • The Chinese Taipei Olympic Committee had to submit a new flag, anthem, and emblem for IOC approval by January 1980. (Chan 481)
    • Chan describes the PRC’s reaction as jubilant. (Chan 481)

    Analysis

    • The naming decision favored the PRC’s claim to represent China and limited the name and symbols under which Taiwan’s athletes could compete.
    • For Beijing, this was a step toward its goal of one China. The arrangement still left Taiwan and the mainland separate.
  14. 1981

    Taiwan signs the agreement

    Source notes

    • Taiwan signed the agreement reluctantly in 1981. (Cheung)

    Analysis

    • With less U.S. backing, Taiwan struggled to keep its previous authority over China’s Olympic representation.
  15. 1984

    Taiwan first competes as Chinese Taipei

    Source notes

    • Taiwan competed under the name Chinese Taipei for the first time in 1984. (Cheung)
  16. 1988

    July

    Taiwan allows athletes to compete in China

    Source notes

    • Taiwan’s government decided its athletes could compete in China under IOC regulations and left those decisions to its national Olympic committee. (Cheung)

    Analysis

    • Taiwan’s decision marked an acceptance of the Olympic arrangement after its earlier reluctance.
  17. 1989

    Taiwan permits travel for official sports events

    Source notes

    • Taiwan’s government made travel to China legal for Taiwanese athletes attending official events run by international bodies that Taiwan belonged to. (Cheung)

    Analysis

    • Athletes could travel for some competitions. The limits on that permission show that Taiwan and China were still separate.
  18. 2020

    Retrospective

    Beijing looks back at the 1958 break

    Source notes

    • In the 2020 account, the PRC attributed its 1958 break with the IOC to what it called a mistake in recognizing a body it described as a local Chinese organization. (International Olympic Committee)
    • The PRC argued that the Taiwan committee should use the name Chinese Taipei Olympic Committee. (International Olympic Committee)
    • The PRC said that it had enjoyed sound cooperation with the IOC since the Chinese Olympic Committee’s legal status in the IOC was restored. (International Olympic Committee)

    Analysis

    • Calling the decision a mistake sounds more restrained than the pressure the PRC applied when tensions were highest.
    • The account puts responsibility for the conflict on the IOC and emphasizes later cooperation. The PRC maintained its claim that Taiwan was part of one China.

Related notes

Dong and Brundage

Source notes

  • The PRC’s account describes Dong explaining Taiwan’s history to Brundage in connection with American history. (“‘Two Chinas’”)

Analysis

  • The account portrays Brundage as hostile to the PRC and Dong as patient in correcting what he saw as an error.

Matches between the PRC and the U.S.

Source notes

  • Chan links sporting matches between the PRC and the United States to negotiations that culminated in President Richard Nixon’s surprise visit to China the following year. (Chan 475)

Analysis

  • These matches came before U.S. support for Taiwan weakened as relations with the PRC improved.

Taiwan’s athletes prepare to compete in China

Source notes

  • Chang Feng-hsu told reporters the event should proceed peacefully and rationally because it would set the tone for future exchanges. (Cheung)
  • Before departure, junior gymnastics team leader Chan Te-chi introduced the group as the Chinese team and said, We’ll be calling ourselves ‘Chinese Taipei’ at official events once we reach the mainland. (Cheung)

Analysis

  • Chang wanted peaceful sports exchanges. Chan’s use of different names suggests that Taiwan’s athletes were still reluctant to use the official name.

Cross-strait friendships and political pressure

Source notes

  • Cheung writes, Many cross-strait friendships were formed, but the political suppression of Taiwan continued. (Cheung)

Analysis

  • Sports exchanges helped build relationships. Taiwan still faced pressure over the IOC’s rules and the PRC’s insistence on following them.
